# What a LangQuest tutorial owes the person watching

A person opens a tutorial because they want something done: their team's words
recorded, a brother's translation made better, a month in the hills without
losing work. The video exists for that person and that goal. It is not a tour of
a screen. Every line should come from concern for them: what do they want, what
stands in their way, and what would they ask if a friend were sitting beside them?

Picture the viewer. They hold a modest phone, perhaps with little data. English
may be their third language, or the video is a machine translation of English.
They may be nervous of breaking something that belongs to the team. They can
pause, but they should not have to.

## The seven promises

1. **Their goal first.** The opening says what the viewer will be able to do and
   why they would want to, in their terms. "Here's how to record a passage" names
   the feature; a goal says what it gets them.
2. **A reason for each step that is not obvious.** Say what a step gets the
   person, not only how the app behaves. "Pause between parts: each part is kept
   by itself" gives the mechanism. "…so you can fix one part without saying it all
   again" gives the reason to care.
3. **The job, not only the buttons.** Where the work takes judgment, say what good
   work looks like: what to listen for, what to prepare, when to choose which
   option.
4. **The real phone.** The film runs in a clean browser with no permission
   prompts, a fast network and no real camera. Where a real phone will show the
   viewer something the film does not, say so.
5. **A way out of trouble.** Name the one or two likeliest ways this goes wrong
   and the way back. Say what can be undone, so nobody fears trying.
6. **A clear finish.** The viewer knows they are done, what happens next, who is
   waiting on what, and where to go from here.
7. **Easy to follow.** One idea to a line. Common words. Time to look before the
   finger moves. No list read aloud without saying why it matters. Made-up people
   are introduced by what they do ("your team leader, here Daniel").

## Limits

- **Truth first.** Every claim is checked against the app. Labels are quoted
  exactly as the screen shows them.
- **Short.** A video grows only where the viewer would otherwise fail. Pay for
  an added reason by cutting a line that only reads the screen aloud.
- **Translatable.** No idiom, no wordplay. Each line must survive machine
  translation into 14 languages.
- **One video, one goal.** Point to another video rather than cover its job.
- **Keep the recorded rules.** No promises of change, nothing that shows or
  explains the phone's disguise (decision 171), and no suggestion of explanatory
  text on the app's screens. The teaching lives in the video.

## How to score a video

For each promise: **Met**, **Partly**, **Missing** or **Not needed** (a promise
that does not fit this video's job). Then give the one change that would most
help this viewer reach their goal, quoting the line it touches. Rank a gap by one
question: would this viewer fail at what they came to do, or only understand less?
